# Dioscorea rotundata Poir. 'Pona': facts for a procedural model

Researched 2026-11-05 for a white Guinea yam grown stage by stage, from a seed yam in a mound to a vine on its stake
and the tuber under it. Built on the template `datasheet.md` and set out like `banana-dwarf-cavendish.md`, with the
template's sections adapted to a yam: planting material and sprouting replace seed and germination, vine and tuber
replace trunk and fruit.

**Labels used below**

- **[CV]**: measured or described on 'Pona' itself (also as 'CRI-Pona', the clean-stock selection of the landrace
  registered in Ghana in 2005/2015, or 'true Pona').
- **[SP]**: said of *D. rotundata* (white Guinea yam), of the Guinea yam group (*D. rotundata* + *D. cayenensis*), or of
  section *Enantiophyllum*.
- **[EST]**: my estimate for modelling, with the reasoning given. It is not a published value.
- **[2nd]**: I read only an abstract, a search snippet, a website table, or a review that cites the work.

Each number has a source tag such as S1. The full references are at the end. Units are metres, centimetres,
kilograms and days. Ages run from **emergence** (MAE = months after emergence) unless a row says "after planting"
(MAP). In Ghana a Pona sett is often planted in the dry season and sits in the mound for 1–3 months, so MAP and MAE
can differ by that much.

---

## 0. The cultivar in one paragraph

- **What it is.** 'Pona' is "the most popular landrace cultivar of yams on the market in Ghana". The authentic Pona
  "has unique rheological properties including aroma and taste", is "the choicest on both local and foreign markets"
  and is "given premium value", and it is "not easily discernible by morphological characters": it is only known
  after cooking. Sellers pass off other white yams as Pona. [CV] S10
- **Its group.** SSR markers split 72 "Pona" accessions into four groups: True Pona, Larebako, Muchumudu and Dente,
  plus hybrid Pona and hybrid Larebako. All IITA checks fell into the authentic Pona group. 'Mankrong Pona' is a
  separate improved clone. [CV] S10
- **Why farmers grow it.** In Techiman (Bono East, the forest–savanna transition), Pona was the most popular cultivar,
  chosen for **early maturity**, taste, market and yield. [CV] S19 [2nd]. It **does not store**: "Pona cannot be
  stored for more than 3 months". [CV] S11 [2nd]. It is the yam that is **double-harvested** ("milked"): first ware
  yams 5–6 months after emergence, then seed yams 2–3 months later. [CV] S11 [2nd]
- **Rules for the model.**
  - One annual stem from one sett, twining up one stake.
  - One tuber, sometimes two, a year (Ghana checks average 2.0 tubers per plant: S8). It grows down from a small corm
    ("head") at the base of the stem.
  - The plant is grown from tuber pieces and is sterile in practice. Never give it fruit by default (§ Flowers).
  - Everything above ground is annual: it dies back 6–7 months after emergence and the tuber rests 2–3 months.

| Dormancy of Pona | short: it "cannot be stored for more than 3 months" because it sprouts in the barn. Use **60–75 d** | [CV] S11 [2nd]; [EST] from S6's range |

Model notes:

- **Planting in the dry season.** In the Guinea savanna and the forest–savanna transition, white yams are planted
  "in November to December", "where they break dormancy in the mound before sprouting with the rains" (S11). In the
  north, 57 % of farmers planted in November–December and 36 % in March–April (S15). For the model, the seed yam lies
  10–20 cm deep in the mound for 1–3 months before the shoot shows.

## 3. Vine and form

### Twining direction (read this before modelling the helix)

- **What the sources say.**
  - *D. rotundata* stem is "twining to the right" (PROTA, S1; FAO EcoCrop, S2).
  - "Vines that twine to the right, i.e. in clockwise direction **when viewed from the ground upwards**, characterize
    members of section *Enantiophyllum*" [SP] S4.
  - IITA writes "twine to the right (clockwise)", citing Onwueme 1978, without saying from where it is seen [SP] S5.
  - Burkill (Flora Malesiana) draws it: fig. 4c, *D. laurifolia* (sect. *Enantiophyllum*) "showing a stem which twines
    to the right", against fig. 4a, *D. bulbifera* "twines to the left" [SP] S3. I rendered the plate and read the
    helix (crop saved beside this file as `burkill-fig4c-twines-right.png`).
- **What it means geometrically.**
  - In Burkill's drawing of "to the right", the strands of stem passing **in front** of the support rise from
    **lower left to upper right** ("/"). That is a **Z-helix**: a **right-handed helix**, threaded like an ordinary
    screw.
  - On a right-handed helix, a point climbing upward goes **anticlockwise as seen from above**, looking down on the
    stake top. It goes **clockwise as seen from below**, looking up the stake. The second view is exactly what S4 says.
  - So the sources agree once the viewpoint is fixed. They are not in conflict.
- **For the model:**
  - Pona twines **anticlockwise seen from above**. It is a right-handed (Z, "dextrorse" in the modern helical sense)
    helix.
  - Its front strands rise to the right.
  - It is the same hand as beans and most twiners, and the opposite of hops and *D. bulbifera* / *D. dumetorum* /
    *D. esculenta* (all "to the left").
  - In three.js (right-handed, y up), the stem point at height y is `(r cos θ, y, −r sin θ)` with θ **increasing**
    as y grows (θ = 2π·y / pitch). Check: seen from above (camera on +y, x to the right, −z up the screen), that path
    runs anticlockwise. In the right-handed frame (x, −z, y) it is the standard right-handed helix
    `(cos θ, sin θ, cθ)`. Equivalently `(r cos θ, y, r sin θ)` with θ **decreasing** as y grows. Pitch on a 3–5 cm
    stake: about **8–15 cm** per turn, steeper (20–30 cm) when the vine wraps a thicker pole or another vine. [EST,
    derived; pitch estimated from photos]
- **The prompt's question, answered.** It is **not** clockwise seen from above. "Clockwise" in yam texts means seen
  from below, or is left unqualified (S4, S5). The section's character is fixed, so every Pona vine twines the same
  way. [SP] S3: "the direction of twining … is characteristic of whole sections".

| Milking / double harvest | first harvest **4–5 months after emergence** (S1), or "between five and six months after emergence" for Pona (S11), or "5–6 months after planting" (S14). The soil is drawn back, the tuber is cut off **below the head**, leaving the head and roots, and the soil is replaced. The second harvest comes **2–3 months later**, after the shoot senesces, and gives seed yams. First-harvest tubers have "a high water content". | What the model shows after milking | the vine stays green; the mound is opened and re-closed (a darker, freshly heaped side); 2–3 months later 1–3 small seed yams of **0.1–0.5 kg** hang from the old head | [EST] from S1, S14 |
