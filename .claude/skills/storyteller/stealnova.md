# How StealNova makes a future feel inspiring and believable

A study of Matt Madigan's channel @StealNova (https://www.youtube.com/@StealNova), made 2026-11-03 at Samuel's ask:
"really, really good in storytelling and visionary inspiration about the future". Its subject is AI, which a maiaCITY
story never talks about (`SKILL.md`, law 7). We take the craft, never the subject.

## What was studied, and how far it is verified

- **The channel:** relaunched on 7 Oct 2026 after years away; ten narrated films of 8–26 minutes in four weeks; 71.3K
  subscribers on 3 Nov 2026. 2D illustrated motion graphics with heavy glow, one colour world per film, a city skyline
  on almost every horizon, tiny people beside huge things, burned-in captions, a dated source list under every film.
- **Numbers** come from the channel's public feed (views, publish dates, descriptions with chapters) as of 3 Nov 2026.
- **One film's narration is quoted:** "The Singularity is Here... Will Everyone be Rich Soon?" (rg-o7pbZ4R4), from a
  podcast that replayed it almost whole (Creating Wealth ep. 2490), machine-transcribed. Times below are the
  podcast's. YouTube blocked the watch pages and captions, so no other film's first spoken lines or first seconds were
  seen; their openings are read from chapter titles and descriptions.

## The one verified opening, beat by beat

1. **A promise to "you", on a clock:** "[Ten] years from now you'll be living better than the richest person on earth
   lives today."
2. **A credible voice agrees**, then who that voice is, then the turn back to *you*: "he isn't talking about the rich,
   he's talking about you."
3. **The fear, at its strongest, from its strongest advocate:** "most people don't believe that…"
4. **The loop said out loud:** "So that's the fear… And in a few minutes I'll show you why it's a lot less likely than
   it sounds."
5. **Guided imagination, flashy first, then the everyday:** "But first picture what's coming. A machine that builds
   whatever you describe… But the bigger change is the boring stuff. Groceries, rent, a visit to the doctor,
   childcare."
6. **The obvious answer knocked down, with a ladder:** "Your money buys ten times more. Then a hundred times more.
   Eventually a thousand times more."
7. **Proof from history, at human scale:** "In 1836 the richest man in the world died from an infection. A few dollars
   of antibiotics would have saved him… You already live better than every king who ever lived."
8. **A second loop:** "…something nobody saw coming. We'll get there."
9. **Only then the onboarding:** "If this is the first video of ours you've seen, here's why this is happening now."
   That is where our fixed trailer sits: after the hook, before the story.

## How the future is made believable

- **Precedent before prophecy.** Every claim stands on something that already happened: 1836 and antibiotics; child
  mortality in Bangladesh, "one in four… today about three in a hundred"; Alaska's oil dividend since 1982.
- **Nature as proof of possibility.** Chapters titled "Nature Already Does It", "Why a Newborn Doesn't Wither",
  "Nature got here first". If nature already does it, the future is only catching up.
- **Lineage.** "The dream is old": the idea's history makes it feel inevitable rather than invented.
- **The sceptic's best case, then a test, then a concession.** "It's a real fear and smart people take it seriously.
  But look at what it would take…" Then the true part conceded: "None of this is guaranteed." "It isn't automatic."
  Then responsibility handed back to people: "That part isn't up to the machines. It's up to us." Doubt priced in
  makes the hope credible.
- **Claims labelled.** "Projections about the future are estimates, not guarantees"; an ILLUSTRATION tag on imagined
  scenes; chapters called "What's real today".
- **One number per beat, turned into people.** "That's close to $400,000 for every Norwegian." A tower of 722 papers
  beside one tiny figure.

## How it moves between the epic and the human

- The big claim always lands on one person, one object, one household: a mother loading a washing machine, "and now we
  can go to the library", then "That's the real gift of machines. Not just more stuff. More time."
- People as the point: "A meal cooked by someone who loves you. A teacher who knows your name. A night out with your
  friends." "Abundance doesn't make people less valuable. It makes people the point."
- On screen: tiny round-faced people against vast things (a figure on a hill under a beam to the moon).

## How awe is built

- **Ladders:** ten times, a hundred, a thousand; "10 years from now… 15 years from now… 20 years from now…"
- **Sensory postcards:** "Watch the earth rise from the moon on a trip that costs what a plane ticket costs today."
- **The big question as the peak:** "What do we want to become?"
- **"Imagine" as a mode switch:** serif title cards ("Imagine", "Imagine, Again"), captions built from the viewer's own
  memories: "Now think about the best you've ever felt." Once or twice a film, at act breaks.

## Structure

- A cold open of 1–3 minutes; chapters every 43–109 s on average, most titled as a question or a promise.
- Loops promised aloud, paid 10–15 minutes later by name: "Now, back to that check."
- Each chapter: a concrete case first, then the general law.
- Countdowns worst to best ("#12 The Last Invention" up to "#1 The Universe Wakes Up"), then a 13-second "The Climb":
  a fearful topic turned into an ascent.
- A short coda (13–60 s) that echoes the opening line and names the film's idea: "Somewhere in a dark room in 1836…
  You were born right at the start of it. Welcome to the age of abundance."
- The next film is planted in earlier ones: the "matter compiler" is a chapter on 15 Oct and a beat on 20 Oct before
  its own film on 2 Nov.

## The voice

- About 200 words a minute; sentences of 9–10 words; many fragments: "Hands and minds." "Not one miracle. A thousand."
- Lists of three: "Health, time, people you love."
- Questions as transitions: "So how do things actually get cheap? Not by wishing."
- Asides and callbacks: "Stop and think about that for a second." "Remember Keynes?"
- First person rarely, for a personal stance only.
- It works with eyes closed: a podcast replayed a whole film as audio.

## Twelve rules for maiaCITY

1. **Promise the viewer's own future, on a clock.** Say *you*, and give a horizon: the first year, the first fruit (never a day number).
2. **Wonder is the frame; the hard part is a chapter.** Tired land, drowning roots and failures are beats inside, never
   the title.
3. **The title makes a claim; the thumbnail shows the end state in two or three huge words** that are not the title.
   A phrase that worked is reused.
4. **State the sceptic's best case early, test it, concede the true part.** *"A papaya in a city? It drowns in two wet
   days."* Then what it would take, then the honest limit.
5. **Believable through precedent and nature, never adjectives.** Forests already rebuild themselves after a gap
   (succession); the papaya is already a pioneer of open ground. Nature already does it; we follow.
6. **Land the epic in the everyday and in people.** The dome village is the spectacle; fruit picked on the way out of
   the door is the change.
7. **One number per beat, human-sized and physical:** "taller than you in a year", not "2.5 m in 12 months".
8. **Open loops out loud, pay them on schedule, echo the opening at the end, plant the next film.** "By the end
   you'll know why…", then "So, back to that papaya."
9. **"Imagine" is a mode switch**, built from the viewer's own memories, once or twice a film, at the act breaks.
10. **A new beat every 45–110 seconds in long films, opened by a question;** number a long sequence ("three things
    have to go right").
11. **Label what's real and what's imagined:** IN THE GAME / FOR REAL on screen, exactly our premise.
12. **Write for the ear:** short sentences, fragments, lists of three, callbacks. Keep the pace, but give the intimate
    moments more air than his 200 words a minute.

## What we never take

- **The subject.** AI, machines doing it for us. Our stories are people and nature building.
- **Promise inflation.** "LIVE FOREVER", "YOU'LL BE RICH", with the hedge only in the fine print. A maiaCITY hook is a
  promise the film keeps.
- **Borrowed authority.** "MIT on…" with no MIT source named.
- **News-cycle titles** ("Just Arrived", "This Week"): our films must still hold up in years.
- **Dread and politics pivots, celebrity and news clips.** Off our register, and a rights risk.
- **Personal-optimisation advice as the ending.** Ours ends on the city's next step and the viewer's place in it.
